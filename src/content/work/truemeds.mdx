## The ambition

TrueMeds wanted to move beyond being an app people opened only to buy medicine. The larger ambition was to feel more like the pharmacist people already trust — someone who can help them find what they need, explain a lower-cost alternative, and make saving money feel safe rather than risky.

I joined the project as Lead UX Designer for a six-month redesign engagement. I worked with two designers as part of a six-person team, leading the UX direction while staying hands-on through the audit, research and redesign.

## The cheaper medicine wasn't the hard sell. Trust was.

TrueMeds already had users coming into the product, but people were dropping at multiple points between discovery and purchase.

When a substitute was available, showing a lower price was not enough to make someone switch from a medicine they already knew.

The business wanted stronger conversion, higher substitute adoption and a better overall buying experience.

**The design question:** How do you make a cheaper alternative feel like advice from a trusted pharmacist, rather than just another discount?

## From an online pharmacy to a trusted pharmacist

We reframed the experience around three questions:

- **Can I find what I need quickly?**
- **Can I trust what TrueMeds is recommending instead?**
- **Is the saving worth switching for?**

This became a more useful lens for the redesign than treating every screen as an isolated UX problem.

## What changed our view of the problem

Our audit, stakeholder inputs, user sessions, app-review analysis and competitor benchmarking pointed to three recurring behaviours.

### Trust came before price

Users questioned whether the substitute would have the same effect, whether the composition was comparable, who manufactured it, why it cost less and whether it was safe to switch. **Price created interest. Confidence created action.**

### The decision was not linear

Users could need awareness on Home, reassurance during Search, comparison on the product page, and a reminder of value in Cart. Instead of creating one substitute-education flow, we distributed the right message across the moments where users were already making a decision.

### Savings needed context

A percentage discount alone was easy to ignore. Savings became more meaningful when users could understand what they had already saved, what they could save on the current order and why the substitute was cheaper.

**This shifted the redesign from "promote substitutes harder" to "build confidence across the journey."**

## 01 — Make the home respond to user behaviour

The existing home worked largely as one long catalogue of banners, categories, products and content. Important actions competed for attention, sections became repetitive, personalisation was limited and the substitute proposition could disappear among everything else on the page.

So we stopped thinking about Home as one fixed experience, and designed around three user states instead: someone new to TrueMeds, someone who already uses substitutes and wants to reorder quickly, and someone who keeps buying the medicine they know and has never tried one.

The home became less about showing more content and more about showing the **right reason to act**.

## 02 — Reduce the perceived risk of switching

The original product page could show that a substitute was cheaper, but users still had to work to understand whether switching made sense.

We reorganised the experience around the decision itself. The searched medicine and its substitute became clearly distinct states. The substitute experience brought forward composition, manufacturer, pack details, price difference and comparison.

We also explored explaining **why substitutes can cost less**, rather than expecting users to trust a large discount without explanation. **Price gets attention. Explanation builds confidence.**

We also designed for medicines with no substitute available instead of forcing the recommendation pattern everywhere.

## 03 — Make savings visible without slowing down checkout

By the time someone reaches Cart, they are no longer exploring. They are trying to finish the order.

The existing cart mixed substitute decisions, discounts, rewards, coupons and checkout information across a long experience. That created more thinking at exactly the point where the journey should become simpler.

We redesigned the cart around three questions: what am I buying, when will I receive it, and what am I actually saving? Medicine, quantity and price became easier to verify. Delivery estimates moved closer to the order itself. Coupons and rewards were grouped more clearly. The bill explained the relationship between cart value, discounts, rewards, delivery and the final amount payable.

Savings became part of the order itself: **"You saved ₹X on this purchase."** The closer the customer moved toward payment, the simpler the experience became.

## One strategy across the journey

**Home** created relevance. **Product pages** built confidence. **Cart** made the value tangible.

**Help me see it → Help me trust it → Help me act.**

That gave the team a consistent way to evaluate new ideas without forcing every user through the same linear education flow.

## Outcome

Several recommendations from the audit were implemented, while broader parts of the redesign were taken forward selectively as the product evolved.

The work helped move the product toward a clearer direction: not simply selling medicines at a lower price, but making the value of substitute medicines easier to understand and trust throughout the buying journey.
